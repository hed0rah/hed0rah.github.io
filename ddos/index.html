DDoS Field Manual

Prehistory: Before It Had a Name

Denial of service predates the internet's commercial era by two decades. The through-line is constant: a resource that is cheap to request and expensive to serve, and no mechanism that makes the requester prove cost.

YearEventWhy it matters
1974David Dennis, 13, sends the ext command to PLATO terminals at CERL that have no external device attachedCommonly cited as the first DoS. A malformed but legal command forces a hang. Amplification factor of one, but the principle is there
1988Morris worm. Reinfection probability 1-in-7 means hosts run many copiesNot designed as DoS. The denial of service was an emergent property of uncontrolled replication. Roughly 10 percent of the connected internet down. CERT founded in response
1995Strano Network "Net-Strike" against French government sites. Manual reload protestFirst organised political DoS. Direct ancestor of LOIC and Anonymous
Sep 1996Panix, a New York ISP, is SYN-flooded off the internet for a weekThe first attack that forced a protocol-level fix. Produces SYN cookies (Bernstein and Schenk) and RFC 4987
Why 1996 is the real year zero Panix was the moment the industry learned that a protocol's own state machine could be turned into the weapon. Nothing was malformed. Every packet was a legal TCP SYN. The attack worked because the server was obligated to remember each one.

Malformed Packets, 1996-1998

A short, spectacular window where one packet could take down a host. These were not floods. They were crashes: bugs in reassembly and state handling that a single crafted datagram could trigger. All are dead against modern stacks, and all of them taught something that still applies.

Teardrop, drawn

Normal fragmentation - offsets tile the payload with no gaps

  frag 1   offset=0    len=800    [0 ................ 799]
  frag 2   offset=800  len=800                      [800 .............. 1599]
                                   reassembly: memcpy into buf[offset], len

Teardrop - the second fragment starts INSIDE the first

  frag 1   offset=0    len=800    [0 ................ 799]
  frag 2   offset=600  len=200              [600 . 799]
                                          ^ overlap

  kernel computes   end   = offset + len
                    delta = prev_end - offset      =  800 - 600 = 200
                    len  -= delta                  =  200 - 200 = 0  ... or negative

  len passed to memcpy as a SIGNED int, then cast to size_t
  -N becomes ~4 billion. Copy walks off the end of kernel memory. Panic.
The class never died, only the instances FragmentSmack (CVE-2018-5391) is teardrop's logic applied to the cost of reassembly bookkeeping rather than to a memcpy: send fragments that never complete a datagram, force the kernel to hold and linearly scan the queue. 2018, not 1997. Any protocol that reassembles, reorders, or buffers on the defender's behalf is carrying this risk.

Directed Broadcast: Amplification Is Invented

1997 is when the attacker stopped paying for their own bandwidth. Smurf is the origin of every reflection attack that followed, and the structural lesson has not changed in nearly thirty years: if a small request produces a large answer, and the source address can be forged, you have a weapon.

SMURF, 1997

  attacker              amplifier network 10.0.0.0/24         victim
     |                   (~250 live hosts)                       |
     |  1 ICMP echo request                                       |
     |  dst = 10.0.0.255   (directed broadcast)                  |
     |  src = VICTIM      (spoofed)                            |
     +-------------------------> [router] --> all 250 hosts       |
                                                 |               |
                                 250 echo replies, all addressed |
                                 to the forged source            |
                                                 +-------------->|
                                                                 |
  cost to attacker: 1 packet        cost to victim: 250 packets
  amplification: ~250x, entirely from a routing default
VariantProtocolNote
SmurfICMP echoThe original. Named after the source file smurf.c
FraggleUDP echo (7) and chargen (19)Same trick on the ancient diagnostic services. Chargen is worse: it answers with a stream
The fix that actually worked Two documents ended Smurf. RFC 2644 / BCP 34 (1999) changed the router default so directed broadcast forwarding is off, and RFC 2827 / BCP 38 (2000) told transit providers to drop packets whose source address could not have come from that interface. BCP 34 was adopted almost universally because it was a vendor default flip. BCP 38 requires every network operator to act, benefits other people's networks, and twenty-five years later is still not universally deployed. That asymmetry is the single largest reason reflection attacks are still viable today.

The Amplification Arms Race, 2013-2018

Five years in which the industry rediscovered Smurf once per protocol. Each cycle is identical: someone finds a UDP service that answers small queries with large responses, the internet turns out to have hundreds of thousands of them exposed, the record falls, the service gets filtered or patched, repeat.

35 seconds The 31.4 Tbps record lasted about as long as it takes to read this box. That duration is the defining operational fact of the current era. 90.6 percent of network-layer attacks now end inside ten minutes. Any defence involving a human decision, a phone call, a BGP announcement waiting on convergence, or a support ticket has already lost. Detection-to-mitigation has to be automatic and measured in seconds, which is precisely the SLA language every vendor now competes on.

The Shape of the Curve

Read vertically, the history is not a story about bandwidth. It is a story about which scarce resource is cheapest to exhaust in a given decade.

Two things never changed 1. Source address spoofing is the enabling condition for every reflection attack ever built, and BCP 38 has been the answer since 2000. It is unevenly deployed because the cost falls on one network and the benefit lands on everyone else. It is a tragedy of the commons with an RFC number.
2. Every attack is a statement about asymmetric cost. Find the request that is cheap to send and expensive to answer. Teardrop, Smurf, SYN flood, memcached, Slowloris, Rapid Reset and DNSBomb are all the same sentence in different grammar.

Taxonomy and the OSI Map

Three families. Everything else is a variation, a combination, or a delivery mechanism. The useful question for any vector is not "what layer" but which finite resource does this exhaust, because that determines where it can be stopped.

The bps / pps distinction matters more than the layer A 100 Gbps flood of 1500-byte packets is roughly 8.3 Mpps. The same 100 Gbps in 64-byte packets is roughly 195 Mpps, over twenty times the packet rate. Most routers, firewalls and load balancers have a packet budget, not a byte budget, because every packet costs a lookup regardless of size. This is why small-packet floods take down boxes that are nowhere near their bandwidth limit, and why the pps figure in an attack report is often the more alarming number.

Volumetric Floods

The brute-force family. No cleverness, only capacity. The defence is arithmetic: your absorbing capacity must exceed theirs, so this is the one category where the answer is genuinely "buy a bigger network".

Reflection and Amplification

Two separate properties that are almost always used together and almost always conflated.

Reflection is using a third party to deliver the traffic. It hides the origin and defeats source-based blocking, because the source addresses you see are legitimate servers.
Amplification is the response being larger than the request. It multiplies the attacker's bandwidth.
You can reflect without amplifying (SYN-ACK reflection, BAF ~1). You cannot amplify off a third party without reflecting. Both require source address spoofing, which is why BCP 38 is the root-cause fix for the entire category. 
the generic reflection primitive

   attacker                reflector pool              victim
   (1 Gbps up)            (100k open UDP services)
       |
       |  spoofed query, src = VICTIM
       |  small: 40-80 bytes
       +--------------------> [ ] [ ] [ ] [ ] [ ]
                               |   |   |   |   |
                               |   |   |   |   |   large responses, all
                               |   |   |   |   |   legitimately sourced
                               +---+---+---+---+---------------> |
                                                                 |
   1 Gbps x BAF 55 (DNS) = 55 Gbps at the victim
   1 Gbps x BAF 557 (NTP monlist) = 557 Gbps
   1 Gbps x BAF 10,000 (memcached) = mathematically absurd,
   bounded only by the reflector's own uplink

 key properties for the defender:
   - source IPs are REAL and often important (you cannot blanket-block DNS)
   - traffic arrives from the correct source PORT for that service
     (53, 123, 1900, 11211, 389). port-based filtering is the crude fix
   - the victim never sees the attacker. attribution is near-impossible
     without cooperation from the reflector operators

Requirements for a new amplifier

  • Connectionless. UDP, or a protocol that answers before validating a return path. TCP amplification is hard because the handshake proves the source address.
  • Response larger than request. Ideally by a lot, and ideally controllable by the requester.
  • Deployed at scale on the public internet. A 10,000x amplifier on fifty hosts is a curiosity. A 4x amplifier on ten million hosts is a weapon.
  • No rate limiting by default. RRL (Response Rate Limiting) on DNS servers was the single most effective mitigation ever shipped for this class.
TCP amplification exists anyway TCP Middlebox Reflection (USENIX 2021, Bock et al). Nation-scale censorship middleboxes and misconfigured firewalls respond to a single crafted TCP packet with a block page or an injected RST, without ever completing a handshake. Some respond with an infinite stream. Observed BAF exceeds 100x, with theoretical values in the millions from devices that ignore TCP state entirely. It broke the assumption that TCP is spoof-proof.

Reading the table correctly BAF alone does not rank threat. Protocol and State Exhaustion

Attacks on the defender's memory rather than the defender's bandwidth. The target is any table with a finite number of rows: the SYN backlog, the conntrack table, the firewall session table, the load balancer's connection pool, the TLS session cache.

SYN flood: the half-open state machine, 1996 to today

   attacker                                        server
      |                                               |
      |------------ SYN (spoofed src) -------------->|
      |                                               | allocate TCB
      |                                               | enqueue in backlog
      |<----------- SYN-ACK ------------------------- | sent to the forged src,
      |                                               | which never answers
      |               (nothing)                       |
      |                                               | retransmit SYN-ACK
      |                                               | 5 times over ~75s
      |                                               | entry held the whole time

  backlog of 1024 entries, 75s timeout:
  ~14 SYN/sec saturates it. a modem could do this in 1996.

  SYN COOKIES (Bernstein / Schenk, 1996) - the fix
  encode the connection state INTO the initial sequence number:

    ISN = hash( src_ip, src_port, dst_ip, dst_port, secret, t ) || t || mss_idx
           \_____________ 24 bits ______________/  5b  3b

  server allocates NOTHING. if the ACK comes back, ISN-1 reconstructs
  the state. zero memory per half-open connection.
  cost: MSS is quantised to 8 values, and TCP options (window scaling,
  SACK, timestamps) are lost unless timestamps carry them. this is why
  cookies are usually enabled only under pressure, not always-on.

The descendants of teardrop. These exploit implementation and specification errors rather than capacity, which makes them dangerous out of proportion to the traffic involved: a few thousand packets per second can take a host down.

DNS as Target, Not Carrier

DNS was 34.3 percent of all network-layer attacks in H1 2026 and DNS floods rose from 25.7 to 40.0 percent quarter on quarter. Worth separating carefully: DNS is used both as an amplifier (covered above) and as the victim, and the second case is the one that takes whole platforms offline, as Dyn demonstrated.

Shaping Tactics and Multi-Vector

Modern attacks are rarely one vector. The operational pattern is a campaign: probe the defence, observe what is filtered, rotate. Akamai's 2026 framing calls this the industrialisation of attack campaigns, with DDoS run alongside API abuse and credential attacks rather than in isolation.

Panix, September 1996: The Protocol Bill Comes Due

6 September 1996. Public Access Networks Corporation, one of the oldest ISPs in New York, is knocked off the internet by a sustained SYN flood reported at 150 to 210 packets per second. That rate is almost embarrassing by modern standards. It worked because the TCP backlog was small and the timeout was long.

DetailValue
Rate150-210 SYN/sec, spoofed source addresses, randomised
EffectMail and news servers unreachable. Panix staff fought it around the clock for weeks before it stopped
Published inPhrack 48 and 2600, weeks earlier. The attack followed the publication almost immediately
Direct outcomeSYN cookies, invented independently by Dan Bernstein and Eric Schenk in September 1996. Shipped in Linux 2.1.44 and FreeBSD. RFC 4987 documents the whole class in 2007
Why this case is still taught It is the cleanest example of a design vulnerability rather than a bug. Nothing was malformed, nothing was overflowing, no implementation was wrong. TCP's three-way handshake requires the server to commit memory before the client has proved it exists. The fix, encoding the state in the sequence number so the server commits nothing, is the pattern that QUIC's Retry tokens and DNS cookies both copy. When you see "stateless challenge" in any modern protocol spec, you are looking at Panix's descendants.

MafiaBoy, February 2000: Project Rivolta

Michael Calce, a 15-year-old in Montreal, uses a network of compromised university machines (mostly at UCSB and other institutions with fat, unmonitored pipes) to take down the biggest sites on the web over roughly a week.

  • Feb 7: Yahoo, then the most-trafficked site on the internet, offline for about an hour under an estimated 1 Gbps. That figure was roughly the entire capacity of a large site at the time.
  • Feb 8: Buy.com (on its IPO day), eBay, CNN, Amazon.
  • Feb 9: E-Trade, ZDNet.
  • Caught because he named the targets in an IRC channel under surveillance. Sentenced in Sep 2001 to eight months open custody as a minor.
What actually changed The commonly-quoted 1.2 billion USD damage figure was never substantiated and is best treated as folklore. The real consequences were institutional: a White House cybersecurity summit convened within days, the FBI's NIPC got significant attention and budget, and DDoS moved from a hacker-scene curiosity to a board-level risk. Every commercial scrubbing business traces its market to this week. The attack tooling was trinoo and TFN, downloaded, not written. The skill floor was already near zero in 2000.

Estonia, April 2007: DDoS as Statecraft

Following the relocation of the Bronze Soldier Soviet war memorial from central Tallinn, Estonia, then the most digitally-dependent state in Europe, absorbs three weeks of coordinated attacks.

CoordinatedApr 30 - May 18Rented botnets. Waves timed to symbolic dates, notably May 9, Victory Day. Arbor Networks (Jose Nazario) counted 128 distinct DDoS attacks over the period: 115 ICMP floods, 4 TCP SYN floods, 9 generic traffic floods. Ten of them ran at roughly 90 Mbps for ten hours each

Targets: parliament, the presidency, ministries, the two largest banks (Hansabank and SEB Eesti), the two largest newspapers, and the country's DNS infrastructure. Estonia's response, disconnecting international traffic to preserve domestic service, worked but meant self-imposed isolation.

The consequence that outlived the attack Estonia formally raised the possibility of invoking NATO Article 5. The alliance declined, but the question could not be un-asked. The NATO Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence was established in Tallinn in 2008 and produced the Tallinn Manual, the first serious attempt to map international law onto cyber operations. By modern standards 90 Mbps is a rounding error. The significance was never the bandwidth: it was that a sovereign state's essential services could be suspended by an unattributable actor at negligible cost, with no doctrine for what that was.

HTTP/2 Rapid Reset, 2023

CVE-2023-44487, disclosed 10 October 2023 in a coordinated release by Google, Cloudflare and AWS after roughly two months of in-the-wild exploitation. The most important DDoS event of the decade so far, because it is a flaw in a specification rather than in any product.

ProviderPeakMultiple of their previous record
Google398 Mrps7.5x
Cloudflare201 Mrps~3x
AWS155 Mrps
The number that should worry you Cloudflare's 201 Mrps came from a botnet of roughly 20,000 machines. For scale, they routinely observe botnets in the hundreds of thousands. A 20,000-node botnet producing three times the previous world record means the leverage per node increased by orders of magnitude. The same botnet running a conventional HTTP flood would have been unremarkable. When a protocol gives away asymmetry, bot count stops mattering.

Mitigations, in order of how well they work

  • Count resets, not just concurrency. Track RST_STREAM per connection and send GOAWAY when the ratio of resets to completed streams goes wrong. This is the actual fix and every major server shipped a version of it.
  • Cap total streams per connection over the connection's lifetime, not just concurrently.
  • Charge for cancelled work. If a request has already been proxied upstream, a client-side cancel should not refund the slot.
  • Do not disable HTTP/2. Falling back to HTTP/1.1 is the reflex answer and it trades a specific flaw for worse performance and a different set of exhaustion attacks.

The same class reappeared within months as the Continuation Flood (April 2024): endless CONTINUATION frames with END_HEADERS never set, buffered indefinitely, and in several implementations not logged at all because the request never completed. The lesson generalises: any protocol feature that lets a client create server-side work and then withdraw it without cost is a DDoS primitive waiting to be found.

15 June 2004: The Control Plane

Around 13:30 ET on Tuesday 15 June 2004, Google, Yahoo, Microsoft, MSN, Apple, Lycos and Symantec became unreachable or severely degraded. Keynote measured some at "nearly zero" availability. Akamai carried roughly 15 percent of web traffic at the time. Service was restored by about 15:30 ET, so roughly two hours.

The visible symptom was that akadns.net, Akamai's customer-facing DNS zone, stopped resolving. Chief Scientist Tom Leighton's account: an attack against the authoritative nameservers Akamai ran on behalf of customers, not against the content edge. His framing was that the nameservers never went down, they were performance-degraded, and "our systems performed normally, as they are designed to perform".

The blast radius has never been reconciled Three mutually inconsistent numbers were published. Akamai initially said roughly 50 of its ~1,100 customers. Leighton then told Computerworld it was "a name server attack against four of our customers", and separately that about 4 percent of the customer base was potentially impacted. Four and four percent (about 44) cannot both be true. He added that of those affected, "half of them did not have any noticeable impact".
Attribution: never established, and it was contested at the time No arrest, indictment or named actor, then or since. Bruce Schneier: "My guess is that it [was] some kind of an internal failure within Akamai or maybe a targeted attack against them by someone with insider knowledge and access." Craig Labovitz, then at Arbor Networks, said there was no suspicious traffic or DNS pattern consistent with a massive distributed attack. Leighton rejected the insider theory, calling the attack "sophisticated and very large-scale, but it did not require insider knowledge". Akamai's public framing also narrowed within 24 hours, from "large scale international attack on the Internet's infrastructure" to the customer-nameserver account it stood behind. The root cause remains publicly unconfirmed.

What is not in dispute is the lesson and the lineage. Analysts read it as the moment attackers stopped flooding bandwidth and started attacking the control plane of infrastructure providers. Leighton said Akamai immediately deployed "several additional defensive mechanisms" and called DNS "a critical component of the Internet" and "one of the most vulnerable". Twenty years later that thread runs straight to Edge DNS and, in 2024, to Shield NS53, a product whose stated purpose is absorbing NXDOMAIN water-torture attacks in front of a customer's own nameservers. 13-18 February 2017: Cloudbleed

Uninitialised memory disclosure from the edge HTML parser into third-party responses. Root cause: Ragel-generated C used == instead of >= to test for buffer end. A malformed tag terminating a buffer let the pointer run past the end into adjacent heap, which was emitted in the response body.

  • Latent since 22 September 2016 when Automatic HTTP Rewrites shipped. Greatly amplified on 13 February 2017 when Email Obfuscation moved to the new parser.
  • Reported by Tavis Ormandy (Project Zero) at 00:32 on 18 February. Email Obfuscation globally disabled in 47 minutes, all three triggering features off within seven hours.
  • Triggered 1,242,071 times, roughly 1 in 3,300,000 requests. Fewer than 180 sites could trigger it before 13 Feb; 6,457 after. Cleanup pulled over 80,000 unique cached pages from Google, Bing, Yahoo, Baidu, Yandex and DuckDuckGo over twelve days.
  • Contested severity. Cloudflare's sampling found ~0.44 cookies and zero passwords per leak and argued genuinely sensitive exposure was nearer 12,420 instances. Outside researchers argued the sampling understated real risk, since leaked data was already cached and redistributed, and that any of ~2 million domains could have received another customer's memory. SSL private keys were not exposed.

20 July 2022: 0ktapus, and the best argument for hardware keys ever recorded

The phishing domain cloudflare-okta.com was registered at 22:13:04. The SMS wave fired at 22:49, thirty-six minutes later, from four T-Mobile SIMs, pointing at a cloned Okta login page that relayed credentials and TOTP codes to the operator in real time over Telegram, then pushed a payload bundling AnyDesk.

Over 100 employees targeted. Three entered credentials. Zero accounts compromised. Every Cloudflare employee holds a FIDO2 hardware key. WebAuthn binds the credential to the origin, so the key simply would not sign for cloudflare-okta.com. The stolen password and the stolen TOTP code were both useless. The same operator took Twilio concurrently. This is the cleanest public demonstration that phishing-resistant MFA defeats an adversary-in-the-middle proxy phish, and that TOTP does not.

20 June 2024: an infinite loop with no stack growth

Prolexic deployment variants, current definitions

  • Prolexic Routed: customer advertises a /24 IPv4 or /48 IPv6 via BGP, clean traffic returns over GRE. Always-on or on-demand. This is the variant that failed on 17 June 2021.
  • Prolexic over Akamai Direct Connect (the old "Connect"): private 10G and 100G ports, SLA 99.9 to 99.99 percent.
  • Prolexic IP Protect (the old "Proxy"): per-IP or per-service mitigation without a routable block. For fragmented IP space or cloud-hosted origins.
  • Prolexic On-Prem / Hybrid, powered by Corero: on-premises scrubbing with cloud burst.

Akamai's published current scale: 32 anycast scrubbing centres, 20+ Tbps dedicated DDoS capacity, 225+ frontline responders across six locations, 100 percent platform availability SLA, zero-second mitigation SLA, and a claim of more than 98 percent of attacks blocked instantly. Remember what the zero-second SLA means: proactively configured controls.

What the Ledgers Actually Say

  • The control plane is the target, and has been since 2004. Akamai 2004 was DNS. Dyn 2016 was DNS. DNS is 34.3 percent of network-layer attacks in H1 2026 and over 60 percent of Akamai's 2024 mitigations had a DNS component. Twenty-two years and the answer is still "run two independent providers".
  • Every real compromise came through a third party. Cloudflare 2012 was chained account recovery at AT&T and Google. 2023 was an unrotated Okta-derived credential. 2025 was a Salesloft OAuth token. The edge itself has never been the way in.
  • Configuration is code and mostly is not treated as such. Nearly every major outage at both companies was a config or limit change shipped globally without the staged rollout the code path already had. Cloudflare's post-November-2025 commitment, progressive rollouts for configuration on the same footing as code, is the correct conclusion and it took two outages seventeen days apart to reach it.
  • Attackers respond to hardening by changing shape, not by giving up. The Eastern European customer hardened all twelve datacentres after July 2022, so the attacker went from 512 IPs at one site to 1,813 across six, escalating in sixty seconds. Harden the host and they attack the prefix.
  • The published number is always "largest mitigated by a vendor who publishes". Spamhaus's famous 300 Gbps was secondhand. Cloudflare's two biggest records have no postmortem. Akamai's largest 2026 figures are scoped to one customer's history. The ladder is real and the rungs are softer than they look.
  • Absorbing the attack and deciding who deserves absorbing are the same capability. Krebs in 2016 is the cleanest instance. Nobody has a good answer, both companies have changed their minds publicly, and that is more honest than a policy that was never tested.
Sources worth reading directly Cloudflare publishes genuinely excellent postmortems: the 2 July 2019 regex writeup, the 18 November 2025 outage, the Thanksgiving 2023 incident report and the July 2025 1.1.1.1 postmortem are all worth reading in full and are unusually candid. Akamai's SIRT research blog and the Prolexic mitigation writeups are the best public source on botnet propagation mechanics. GitHub's own 28 February 2018 incident report is the minute-by-minute record of the memcached attack from the target's side.

Four Principles

  • You cannot filter a saturated pipe from behind it. Volumetric mitigation is always upstream. Everything you do on your own equipment applies only to traffic that already arrived, which means the link is already full. This is the one structural fact that determines the whole architecture.
  • Reverse the asymmetry. Every attack is a bet that the request costs the attacker less than the response costs you. Mitigation means making the attacker pay: a stateless challenge, a cookie, a proof of work, a cryptographic handshake, or simply refusing to allocate anything until the source address is proven.
  • Automate below the human timescale. A 35-second attack is over before a pager is answered. Anything requiring a phone call, a ticket, or a BGP announcement that waits on convergence has lost. The only acceptable human role is post-incident.
  • Defence in layers, because attacks are multi-vector. Volumetric absorption upstream, state protection at the edge, behavioural controls at L7. Any single layer alone gets rotated around.

Detection

The hard part is not stopping attack traffic, it is deciding that traffic is an attack quickly enough and without killing a legitimate spike. A product launch, a TV mention, or a viral post look statistically identical to an L7 flood at the first derivative.

The signal that actually separates attack from spike Not volume. Composition. A legitimate spike preserves the existing distribution of paths, user agents, geographies, referrers, cache hit ratio and session depth: more of the same people doing the same things. An attack changes the shape: cache hit ratio collapses, the path mix flattens or concentrates, session depth goes to one request, and the ASN distribution stops matching your user base. Measure the distributions, not the counters.

For volumetric attacks there is no filtering, only capacity and dispersion. The question is how the traffic reaches the capacity.

The GRE and MTU trap Routed scrubbing usually returns clean traffic to you over a GRE tunnel, which adds 24 bytes of header. If path MTU discovery is broken anywhere (and it frequently is, because people block ICMP type 3 code 4), large packets vanish and you get the classic symptom: small requests work, downloads and TLS handshakes with big certificates hang. Clamp MSS on the tunnel. This bites people during their first real incident, when they are least able to debug it.

L7 and Bot Management

At layer 7 every request is individually valid, so the question is never "is this packet malformed" but "is this client who it claims to be, and is this pattern plausible".

Cache hit ratio is a security metric If your edge cache hit ratio is 95 percent, an L7 flood has to be twenty times larger to produce the same origin load. Treat the ratio as a defensive control with a threshold and an alert, not just a performance number. A sudden collapse in hit ratio with flat or rising request volume is one of the cleanest early indicators of a cache-busting attack, and it usually shows up before origin latency does.

Under Attack: Runbook

Before (this is where the work actually is)

  • Inventory every internet-facing asset, including the ones nobody remembers. The forgotten staging box shares an uplink with production.
  • Know your baseline numbers: normal bps, pps, rps, cache hit ratio, origin latency, connection counts, per hour and per weekday.
  • Verify the origin is unreachable except from the CDN. Then check DNS history, certificate transparency logs, and mail headers for leaks of the real address.
  • Two independent DNS providers. Confirm the second is not reselling the first.
  • Pre-build and test mitigation controls. Run a real drill with the provider. An untested runbook is a document, not a capability.
  • Contacts: your provider's emergency number, your transit providers' NOCs, and who is authorised to approve a diversion at 03:00. Keep this off the infrastructure that will be down.

During

  • Confirm it is an attack. Check composition, not volume. Rule out your own deploy, a failing dependency, a cache purge, and a legitimate spike.
  • Classify. Volumetric, state, or application? bps, pps, or rps? That decides who can help.
  • Escalate to the layer that can act. If the pipe is full, only upstream matters. Do not spend the first ten minutes tuning local iptables on a saturated link.
  • Capture evidence while it is happening. Flow records, packet samples, source ASN distribution, target paths, fingerprints. You will not be able to reconstruct this afterwards and you may need it for law enforcement or for an insurance claim.
  • Watch for the smokescreen. Check authentication logs, egress volume, and admin activity. The flood may be the distraction.
  • Do not pay the ransom. Payment marks you as a paying target and the demand does not end.
  • Communicate. A status page on infrastructure that is independent of the affected systems, plus a social channel. Silence during an outage costs more trust than the outage.

After

  • Timeline the incident to the second: first packet, detection, decision, mitigation live, recovery. The gaps are your real SLA.
  • Ask what would have happened if the attack had been ten times larger, or had lasted forty seconds instead of forty minutes.
  • Fix the asymmetry that was exploited, not just the signature. A vector-specific rule gets rotated around within days.
  • If you were used as a reflector, fix that too. Half the internet's amplification capacity is other people's unfinished homework.
