A field guide to how the internet gets attacked and defended today. Attacks split three ways: volumetric (drown the pipe), protocol/state (exhaust a table), and application (make the server do expensive work) - plus bots & abuse, which look like real traffic. The through-line of modern defense is identity: an IP is shared and a User-Agent is a free-text lie, so blocking leans on fingerprints of how a client actually speaks (JA4+). Three questions sort almost any attack: what runs out first (bandwidth, a kernel table, or CPU/backend), what layer it rides, and does it look like real traffic. Volumetric floods drown the link; protocol attacks exhaust finite state (half-open connections, conntrack); application attacks are cheap requests that trigger expensive work. Abuse (bots) is the odd one out - it IS "real" traffic, just unwanted, so you can't drop it by volume, you have to tell it apart.

the asymmetry that shapes everything. The higher the layer, the cheaper it is to attack and the harder to defend: a single crafted L7 request can cost the attacker a few bytes and cost you a database query, a template render, and a backend round-trip. That gap - cheap to send, expensive to serve - is why L7 and bot defense is mostly about identifying the sender, not out-muscling the volume.

SYN floodRFC 4987amplificationreflection off open UDP services

L3/4 is a numbers game against finite resources. A SYN flood sends the opening SYN of a TCP handshake and never completes it; each one pins a slot in the server's half-open-connection table until it times out. An amplification/reflection attack sends a small spoofed-source query to an open UDP service (DNS, NTP, memcached...) so the big answer lands on the victim - the attacker rents a little bandwidth and borrows a lot.

a SYN segment - the half-open primitive

A real TCP SYN to port 443 (source/dest are RFC 5737 doc addresses). The whole attack is this one packet, sent by the million with spoofed sources so the SYN-ACKs go nowhere and the handshakes never finish.

the fix: SYN cookies. Instead of storing half-open state, the server encodes it into the SYN-ACK's initial sequence number (a MAC over the connection tuple + a slow timer) and keeps nothing. A legitimate client's ACK returns that number; the server recomputes and reconstructs the connection from thin air. No table to exhaust (RFC 4987).
a DNS amplification query - small in, huge out

A 36-byte ANY query with an EDNS0 option advertising a 4096-byte UDP buffer. Spoof the source to the victim and the multi-kilobyte answer is delivered to them, not you.

the root cause is spoofing. Reflection only works because the source IP can be forged and the reflector answers whoever "asked." BCP 38 / RFC 2827 ingress filtering at the network edge (drop packets whose source can't legitimately come from that link) would starve every amplification attack at once - which is exactly why it isn't universally deployed.

At L7 the packets are valid HTTP - the damage is in what they make the server do. Floods hammer expensive endpoints (search, login, anything uncached). Slow attacks (Slowloris, slow-read, slow-POST) do the opposite: a trickle of bytes that keeps worker threads/connections pinned open, starving the pool with almost no bandwidth. And HTTP/2's multiplexing opened a new door.

HTTP/2 Rapid Reset - a frame that cancels itself

The client opens a stream (HEADERS) then immediately cancels it with an RST_STREAM. The request is fully processed server-side but never counts against the max-concurrent-streams limit, so one connection can churn tens of thousands of requests/second. This is the RST_STREAM frame: a 9-byte HTTP/2 frame header + a 4-byte error code.

Rapid Reset (CVE-2023-44487, 2023). The largest L7 DDoS on record at the time - it exploited a spec-legal behavior, not a bug, so every HTTP/2 implementation was affected at once. Mitigation is counting resets and reused streams per connection and killing abusive ones, plus capping request rate independent of the stream-concurrency limit. Its cousin, the CONTINUATION flood (2024), abuses unbounded header CONTINUATION frames the same way.
slow attacks cost almost nothing. Slowloris can take down a thread-per-connection server from a single laptop - the defense is event-driven servers, aggressive header/body timeouts, and a reverse proxy that buffers the full request before ever touching the origin.
the hard partit looks like real trafficyou can't drop by volume

Abuse traffic is well-formed, low-rate, and often distributed across huge residential proxy pools (real home IPs rented from malware/SDK networks), so IP reputation and rate limits barely dent it. You defend by telling the automated client apart from the human one.

the modern twist: AI crawlers. A wave of LLM training and retrieval bots now scrapes at volumes that read like a soft DDoS - many ignore robots.txt, rotate residential IPs, and forge browser User-Agents. They are the clearest current case for fingerprint-based identification: the UA says "Chrome," the TLS/HTTP fingerprint says "a Python library."
problemIP is shared, UA is a free-text lieanswerJA4+ fingerprints

You can't block by IP (CGNAT, proxies, shared clouds put thousands of users behind one address) and you can't trust the User-Agent (it's a client-set string - a scraper claims to be Chrome for free). So defenders fingerprint how the client speaks: the exact shape of its TLS handshake, HTTP/2 settings, and TCP options - things a library reveals whether or not it lies about its name.

a JA4 fingerprint, decomposed

JA4 (the TLS ClientHello half of JA4+) hashes the negotiated capabilities into a stable, human-skimmable string. This is real Chrome:

t13d1516h2_8daaf6152771_02713d6af862
t13d1516h2the readable part: t=TCP, 13=TLS 1.3, d=SNI present, 15 cipher suites, 16 extensions, ALPN h2
8daaf6152771sha256[:12] of the sorted cipher-suite list
02713d6af862sha256[:12] of the sorted extensions + signature algorithms
why JA4 beat the old JA3. JA3 hashed raw, order-sensitive fields into one opaque MD5 - trivially perturbed by GREASE and TLS extension-shuffling, so a client could randomize its own fingerprint per connection. JA4 sorts before hashing (defeating shuffling), strips GREASE, and keeps the human-readable prefix, so it's stable across sessions and skimmable by an analyst. The wider JA4+ suite adds JA4H (HTTP), JA4T/JA4TS (TCP), JA4S (the server's hello), and JA4X (the certificate).
the catch: fingerprints spoof too. Tools like curl-impersonate and utls let a bot reproduce a real browser's exact ClientHello - so a fingerprint match is strong evidence, not proof. The state of the art combines the fingerprint with behavior (timing, ordering, whether it solves a challenge) rather than trusting any single signal - the same "obscurity is a layer, not the control" lesson from address scanning.

principledefense in deptheach layer removes work before the next

No single control stops everything; you stack cheap filters in front of expensive ones so each layer sheds load before the next. Roughly outside-in:

the layers
edge / networkAnycast spreads the load across many sites; scrubbing centers absorb volume; BGP flowspec + RTBH (remote-triggered blackhole) drop floods upstream; CDNs serve cached bytes so attacks never reach origin
transportSYN cookies, connection + rate caps, conntrack tuning - survive the state-exhaustion class
applicationWAF rules, rate limiting (token-bucket / sliding-window), challenges (JS, CAPTCHA, proof-of-work), fingerprint + reputation blocking, bot management
architecturalcache everything cacheable, degrade gracefully under load, tarpit + honeypot the obvious offenders, autoscale with a cost ceiling
challenges shift the cost back. A proof-of-work challenge (e.g. Anubis, mCaptcha) makes every client burn a few CPU-milliseconds before it's served - invisible to one human, ruinous to a bot making millions of requests. It's the mirror image of the L7 asymmetry: finally make the attacker pay per request.
rate limiting is a shape, not a number. Token-bucket allows bursts then throttles; sliding-window smooths; per-fingerprint or per-account limits beat per-IP ones when the attacker is spread across a proxy pool. The honeypot angle ties in here - a decoy endpoint or address (see the ipv6-fun canary) turns any hit into a high-confidence "this is hostile" signal to feed the other layers.

The economics are lopsided and that's the whole story. DDoS is a service - "booter/stresser" sites rent floods by the hour for pocket change, and residential-proxy networks sell millions of real IPs. Defense is anycast capacity, scrubbing contracts, and engineering time. The attacker pays cents; the defender pays a retainer.

obscurity is never the control. Hiding an address, a subdomain, or a fingerprint buys time and cuts noise, but anything discoverable will be found and anything static will be predicted - the same lesson whether you're hiding an IPv6 host or a client fingerprint. Layers you can reason about (rate, identity, challenge, capacity) are the actual defense; obscurity just thins the crowd first.
the cat-and-mouse won't end. Better fingerprinting (JA4) begets better spoofing (uTLS/curl-impersonate) begets behavioral + multi-signal detection begets... the durable posture isn't a silver bullet, it's raising the attacker's cost and your own observability faster than they adapt. Detect, identify, mitigate - then watch what changed and do it again.
